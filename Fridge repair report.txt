the explanation of the technician found :
**Overview: How the Module Works**

The power module's job is to take raw AC electricity from the wall and safely turn it into a stable 12V DC power supply for the motherboard:

1. **Conversion:** The **bridge rectifier** turns incoming AC electricity into high-voltage DC electricity.
2. **Control & Switching:** A **PWM controller chip** acts like a rapid light switch, chopping that high-voltage DC power on and off tens of thousands of times a second to feed a transformer safely.
3. **Feedback:** An **optocoupler** uses an internal light signal to check the output voltage on the 12V side and tell the controller chip on the high-voltage side whether to speed up or slow down.

---

**What Went Wrong**

Instead of a complete component breakdown (a "dead short") that would immediately blow a fuse, a **micro electrical leakage** developed inside the module—meaning current began escaping through a tiny, unwanted path.

This tiny leak drained away the small amount of voltage the controller chip needs just to stay powered on, or it tricked the optocoupler into sending false information. Thinking something was unsafe, the controller chip automatically turned itself off to protect the system, preventing the board from producing any 12V power.

---

**Simplified Recap for Your Report**

> The motherboard failed to produce 12V output because a tiny electrical leak developed inside the power module. This leak continuously drained critical control voltages and threw off the circuit's internal timing signals. As a result, the main controller chip automatically went into safety shutdown mode to protect the board, keeping the 12V power line completely turned off.

- In single-evaporator top-mount designs like the Samsung RT32 series, the evaporator coil is located exclusively in the freezer compartment. Cold air is actively pushed down to the fresh food section via an evaporator fan and ductwork. Unlike dual-evaporator systems that feature dedicated cooling coils for each compartment, a single-coil layout means the lower fridge compartment is entirely dependent on clear air passages from above. If a failure in the defrost circuit—whether caused by an open heater, a blown thermal fuse, a drifted NTC sensor, or a failed PCB relay—prevents automatic ice removal, frost will build up until it chokes the evaporator fins and blocks the return duct. This cuts off cold airflow completely, causing the fridge compartment to warm up to room temperature while the freezer struggles at degraded performance. When diagnosing this fault, always verify the 5-blink PCB defrost error, test the continuity of the heater loop components, and ensure the bimetal safety and thermal fuses remain intact before replacing the main control board.

lessons learned :
A confident "I don't know yet" is more valuable than a guess. The turning point in this diagnosis wasn't a lucky guess — it was refusing to accept "everything I tested is fine" as a dead end, and reframing it as a clue pointing to a shared upstream cause.
Isolate before you condemn. Both the fan and the LED tested as "faulty" when judged only by the board's behaviour, and both turned out to be completely healthy once tested in isolation. This is a generally reusable diagnostic principle for any electrical system, not just fridges.
Not every failure leaves a visible sign. The instinct to look for a burnt or bulged component is a good first move, but it isn't sufficient — some of the most disruptive failures (like SMPS capacitor micro-leakage) are only detectable through electrical measurement, not inspection.
Knowing where your competence ends is part of the skill, not a failure of it. Correctly diagnosing the fault down to the exact board and failure mode — and then handing off the mains-side rework to someone with the right tools and experience — produced a better outcome (and a faster, cheaper repair) than either calling a technician blind on day one, or attempting board-level SMPS rework without the right equipment.
Document as you go. The notes taken during diagnosis (blink codes, voltage readings, what was tested and when) are what let the specialist independently confirm the same conclusion quickly, instead of re-doing the whole diagnostic process from scratch.

this is the Refrigerator Defrost Troubleshooting (Complete Guide) :
  
 1. Mechanical Defrost Timers (Older / Basic Units)
Legacy top-mount and simple single-compressor refrigerators rely on a physical mechanical defrost timer.

How it works: A small motorized gear set rotates continuously during compressor operation. Every 6 to 12 hours, the timer mechanically trips a switch that cuts power to the compressor and sends line voltage to the defrost heater.

Characteristics: Found in non-inverter, purely electro-mechanical units. Technicians can manually advance these timers by turning a flathead screw on the timer housing until it clicks into the defrost mode in order to test the defrost system is working properly.

2. in the newest models like mine :

* **Cooling Mode:** The Main Control PCB reads cabinet air thermistors and signals the Inverter PCB to drive the variable-speed compressor. The evaporator fan pushes chilled air from the freezer coil down through the ductwork to the fresh food compartment.
* **Defrost Mode Initiation:** Based on compressor run hours and door cycles, the Main PCB pauses the compressor/fan and sends line voltage through the thermal fuse and closed bimetal thermostat to energize the defrost heater.
* **Normal Termination:** The evaporator NTC thermistor monitors coil temperature. Once frost melts and the coil reaches ~8°C to 10°C, the NTC signals the Main PCB to open its relay and stop heating.
* **Safety Backup 1 (Bimetal Thermostat):** If the NTC sensor drifts or fails, the bimetal thermostat mechanically snaps open at ~10°C to 15°C to cut power directly to the heater.
* **Safety Backup 2 (Thermal Fuse):** If the PCB relay welds shut, the non-resetting thermal fuse permanently blows at ~72°C to prevent thermal runaway and cabinet damage.


2. **Step-by-Step Diagnostic Routine:
* **Defrost Heater:** Measure resistance across terminals. Expect **200–400 Ω**. Open circuit ($\infty\ \Omega$) = blown element.
* **Thermal Fuse:** Test for continuity at room temperature. Must read **0 Ω**. Open circuit ($\infty\ \Omega$) = blown fuse (replace fuse and inspect PCB relay).
* **Bimetal Thermostat:** Must read **0 Ω** when frozen below 0°C, and **Open Loop ($\infty\ \Omega$)** at room temperature.
* **NTC Thermistor:** Measure resistance (~5 kΩ at 25°C; ~13 kΩ at 0°C). Erratic values = sensor drift.
